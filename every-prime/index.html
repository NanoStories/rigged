Every Prime

Separate them with commas or spaces. A prime is a whole number bigger than 1 that only 1 and itself divide.

    How the House wins

    1. Multiply every prime on your list together.
    2. Add one. Call the result N.
    3. Divide N by any prime on your list and the remainder is 1, because N is exactly one more than a multiple of that prime. So none of your primes divides N.
    4. Every whole number bigger than 1 is either a prime or a product of primes, so N has at least one prime factor. By step 3, that prime isn’t on your list.

    None of this depended on which primes you wrote down. Every finite list misses a prime, so there are infinitely many of them.

    Euclid wrote down a version of this argument around 300 BC, in Book IX of the Elements. More than two thousand years later, it’s still how most people first see why the primes never run out.

    A common slip is to think N itself must be prime. It doesn’t have to be: 2 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 11 × 13 + 1 = 30,031 = 59 × 509. The House only needs N to have some prime factor that isn’t on your list, and it always does.

    Start with just 2 and keep adding the smallest prime the House hands you, and you get the Euclid–Mullin sequence: 2, 3, 7, 43, 13, 53, 5, 6,221,671, 38,709,183,810,571, 139, … Mathematicians suspect every prime turns up in it eventually, but nobody has proved it. The smallest prime not yet known to appear is 41.
